Running YOLOv8 + TensorRT + ByteTrack on a Jetson

Detecting and tracking road users on an edge device sounds like a solved problem — until you actually try to run YOLOv8 TensorRT with ByteTrack on a Jetson. The public material splits into two kinds: READMEs that assume everything already works, and forum threads where someone's engine file won't load. This post is the middle ground — field notes from our lab's deployed pipeline: what runs, in what order, and which mistakes cost days.

The code is public: YOLOv8_Object_Tracking_TensorRT, built in STAR Lab for object detection, tracking, and conflict point estimation on live video streams. It captures from cameras, files, or RTSP with OpenCV, detects with a YOLOv8 TensorRT engine, tracks with BYTETrack, computes speed, heading, and distance per track, estimates conflict points and time-to-collision, ships results over UDP, and serves a small Flask web app for visualization. Target hardware: Jetson Orin Nano. It builds on nabang1010's pipeline and triple-Mu's YOLOv8-TensorRT exporter — credit where due.

The stack, and why not DeepStream

NVIDIA's DeepStream is the production answer: GStreamer plumbing, nvstreammux, batching, tracker plugins. If you are shipping a fixed pipeline to 500 cameras, use it. For research — custom association logic, TTC experiments, odd input sources — a plain Python loop around a TensorRT engine is easier to change, and the performance penalty at YOLOv8n scale on an Orin is tolerable. That tradeoff is the whole architectural decision.

Step 1 — Export the engine (on the Jetson)

Two viable paths.

The one-liner (current Ultralytics API):

from ultralytics import YOLO
model = YOLO("yolov8n.pt")
model.export(format="engine", quantize=16)   # FP16 engine, built for THIS device

The explicit pipeline (what our repo uses): export ONNX first, then build the engine — with NMS parameters baked into the graph:

python3 export-det.py --weights yolov8n.pt \
  --iou-thres 0.65 --conf-thres 0.25 --topk 100 \
  --opset 11 --sim --input-shape 1 3 640 640 --device cuda:0

python3 build.py --weights models/onnx/yolov8n.onnx \
  --iou-thres 0.65 --conf-thres 0.25 --topk 100 --fp16 --device cuda:0

Baking confidence and IoU thresholds into the exported graph removes a per-frame Python-side NMS decision. At edge frame rates, every millisecond you do not spend in Python postprocessing counts.

The rule that saves a day: a .engine file is not a model format — it is a build artifact for one GPU architecture and one TensorRT/CUDA combination. Build on the Jetson that will run it. Our environment pins CUDA 12.2 and TensorRT ≥ 8.6.2; the Ultralytics TensorRT docs say it plainly: do not treat an .engine file as portable.

Step 2 — Track

The quick path if you just want IDs on screen:

results = model.track(source="rtsp://camera/stream", persist=True,
                      tracker="bytetrack.yaml")

The full lab pipeline runs the engine through our own loop — srcs/yolov8_bytetrack_starlab.py is the entry point — feeding BYTETrack and then the traffic logic. Expect the first engine build and first inference to be slow; steady-state is what matters.

Step 3 — Tracks to traffic metrics

Detection boxes become track IDs; track IDs become trajectories; trajectories become speed, heading, and distance estimates, and pairs of trajectories become conflict points with time-to-collision. The honest difficulty is not the tracking — it is calibration. Pixel geometry to world geometry decides whether your speed estimates are usable. (More on the analysis side in my post on LLM applications in traffic safety.)

Jetson field notes

  • Fix power mode before benchmarking. sudo nvpmodel -m 0 && sudo jetson_clocks. Comparing throttled to unthrottled runs has invalidated many a benchmark.
  • INT8 needs your data. Post-training quantization calibrates on a representative set; Ultralytics' guidance is explicit that calibration is device- and data-dependent. Calibrate with frames from your cameras, on your Jetson.
  • Sanity-check numbers exist. Ultralytics documents Jetson Orin NX 16 GB at ~3.18 ms/image FP16 and ~2.30 ms INT8 (YOLOv8n, batch 8, predict). Treat those as an order-of-magnitude check, then measure your own.
  • RTSP wants its own thread. Sequentially opening, reading, and inferring in one loop hides latency and drops frames. Decouple capture from inference.
  • Flask viz is for debugging. Fine in the lab; do not put it on a pole.

Roadside footage is personal data — faces, plates, gait. Before a clip leaves the device, redact it: PrivateRedact removes identifiers offline, with a local LLM. Edge processing without an upload path is a good start; treat sharing as a separate, deliberate step.

FAQ

Can I copy a .engine file from my PC or another Jetson?

No. Engines are built per GPU architecture and TensorRT/CUDA version. Rebuild on the deployment device (export from .pt/.onnx there).

Is YOLOv8 still the right model?

Newer Ultralytics families exist; YOLOv8 remains fully supported for detection in the current export paths. On Jetson, model size (n/s) and precision usually matter more than family.

Why ByteTrack and not DeepSORT?

ByteTrack associates low-confidence detections instead of discarding them — free recall on small and distant road users, which is exactly where safety analysis cares.

What about INT8 on the Orin Nano?

Possible and documented, but budget calibration time and re-validate mAP on your own data before trusting counts.
