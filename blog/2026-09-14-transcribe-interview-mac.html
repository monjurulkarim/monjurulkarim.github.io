How to Transcribe Interview Recordings on Mac (2026)

If you do qualitative research, journalism, or user interviews, the recording is not the deliverable — the transcript is. And the hour you spend re-typing an interview is an hour you don’t get back.

This guide compares four practical ways to transcribe interview audio on a Mac in 2026: the free tool already in macOS, a command-line model you can run offline, human transcriptionists, and on-device apps that combine the two.

How to transcribe interview audio: the short answer

  • Recorded inside Apple Notes (macOS Sequoia or later)? The transcript is already there — open the note and copy it.
  • Have a recording file and comfortable in Terminal? Whisper is free, offline, and handles 90+ languages.
  • Need publication-grade verbatim with speaker names? A human transcriptionist, roughly $1–2 per audio minute.
  • Regular interviews and want summaries instead of raw text? An on-device app like MeetingMint imports the file and returns key points and action items.

The rest of this guide is the trade-offs behind each line.

What “good enough” transcription means

Before picking a tool, decide what you actually need:

  • Verbatim transcript. Every “um”, false start, and overlap. Needed for discourse analysis; overkill for most other work.
  • Clean-read transcript. Filler words removed, sentences tidied, speaker turns labeled. This is what most researchers and journalists actually quote from.
  • Summary and action items. You don’t need the words at all — you need the decisions and commitments from a 60-minute conversation.

Most of the pain in transcription workflows comes from aiming at the wrong one of these three.

Method 1: Apple Notes — free, built into macOS

Since macOS Sequoia, the Notes app can record audio and produce a transcript with speaker labels, and you can search the text inside the note. No install, no account, no upload — the transcription runs on Apple silicon.

  1. Open Notes and create a note.
  2. Click the record button in the toolbar and start your interview.
  3. Stop when done — the transcript appears under the recording, with speakers separated.
  4. Select and copy the text into your editor of choice.

Good: free, private, zero setup. Fine for one-on-one interviews in a quiet room.

Bad: you must record inside Notes, so it can’t transcribe a recording you already have. Accuracy drops on field recordings, accents outside the training mainstream, and multi-speaker audio. Export is copy-paste, not a clean file.

Apple documents the feature in their Notes for Mac guide — worth a skim for the supported macOS versions.

Method 2: Whisper at the command line — free and offline

Whisper is OpenAI’s open-source speech recognition model, and it runs fine on a Mac with Apple silicon. It handles 90+ languages and works on files you already have — the part built-in tools can’t do.

Install and transcribe:

pip install -U openai-whisper
# ffmpeg is a dependency: brew install ffmpeg

# small = fast and decent; large-v3 = best accuracy, slower
whisper interview_014.m4a --model small --language en

That writes interview_014.txt, .srt, and .vtt next to your file. The audio never leaves your machine.

Good: free, offline, works on existing recordings, strong multilingual accuracy, plain-text output you can script around.

Bad: no speaker labels — a genuine limitation for interviews, since diarization is a separate problem. The large-v3 model wants more RAM than a base MacBook always has free. And it’s a terminal tool; if that sentence stopped you, this method is not for you.

Practical tip for researchers: run --model small first for a same-day draft, and reserve large-v3 for the recordings you’ll actually quote.

Method 3: Human transcriptionists — the accuracy ceiling

Services and freelancers will transcribe interviews for roughly a dollar or two per audio minute, with multi-day turnaround. For a one-hour interview that’s $60–120 and a clean, speaker-labeled, verbatim transcript.

Good: the most accurate option, real speaker labels, handles crosstalk and heavy accents, zero of your time.

Bad: cost scales with volume — a 20-interview study gets expensive fast. Your recordings leave your machine, which matters when the audio contains participant PII; many IRB protocols and institutional review boards expect you to minimize exactly that exposure. Turnaround is days, not minutes.

A middle path: machine-transcribe everything for the early pass, then send only the interviews you’ll quote at length to a human.

Method 4: On-device transcription apps — Whisper without the terminal

The last option is apps that package models like Whisper behind a normal Mac interface: drag in a file, get a transcript, nothing uploaded. This is where “transcribe audio to text on a Mac” usually lands in practice.

Two examples from my own stack, both on-device:

  • MeetingMint (Mac App Store) is built for meetings but imports any audio — a voice memo, an interview, a Zoom recording — and turns it into minutes, key points, and action items in 90+ languages, entirely on your Mac. If your goal is the summary rather than the verbatim text, this skips the transcript step entirely.
  • AudioRecord (Mac App Store) captures the recording side: mic and system audio into one file with a searchable library — useful when the interview source is a call or a playback on the same machine.

The pattern to look for in any app you evaluate: transcription happens locally (check the privacy label — “Data Not Collected” is the tell), it imports existing files, and it exports plain text or SRT rather than locking your transcript in.

FAQ

How long does it take to transcribe an hour of audio?

With on-device Whisper-class models, roughly real-time to a few times faster depending on the model size and your chip — a one-hour interview in well under an hour, often minutes with the smaller models. Human services take 1–3 days per recording.

Can I transcribe an interview for free on a Mac?

Yes. Apple Notes does it if you record inside the app on macOS Sequoia or later, and the Whisper command-line tool handles existing files at no cost. Both run locally.

Does Whisper identify different speakers?

Not by itself. Whisper transcribes speech to text but does not do speaker diarization. For two-speaker interviews people either label turns by hand afterwards or run a diarization tool on top.

Is it safe to upload interview recordings to transcription sites?

Treat uploads as a data-sharing decision, not a convenience toggle. Interview audio often contains personally identifiable information, and many ethics protocols expect you to keep participant data local when a local option exists. On-device transcription avoids the upload entirely.

What audio format works best for transcription?

Whisper and most Mac apps accept common formats directly (m4a, mp3, wav, flac). Clear mono audio at a modest bitrate beats a huge lossless stereo file — the model cares about speech clarity, not fidelity.
