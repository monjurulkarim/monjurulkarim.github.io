How to Blur Faces in a Video (Every Method, 2026)

Maybe you filmed a great clip and a stranger walked through the frame. Maybe it’s dashcam footage, a recorded interview with a minor, or a workplace incident report. Whatever the reason, knowing how to blur faces in a video — properly, so the blur actually stays on the face — is a different skill than blurring a photo, because faces move.

This guide compares four practical ways to blur faces in video in 2026: purpose-built on-device redaction software, a free desktop editor with motion tracking, a one-line ffmpeg command for static shots, and quick mobile apps. For each one you’ll see what it costs, when it holds up, and when it breaks down.

The short answer

  • The face moves, the footage is sensitive, or you do this regularly: use video redaction software with automatic detection and a review step.
  • Already comfortable in a video editor and it’s a one-off: DaVinci Resolve’s free tier does manual tracking well.
  • Locked-off camera, face never leaves one spot: one ffmpeg command is free and fast.
  • Short social clip, nothing sensitive: a mobile app is fine — but check where the video gets uploaded.

The rest of this guide is the trade-offs behind each line.

Method 2: A free editor with motion tracking (DaVinci Resolve)

DaVinci Resolve’s free tier is a real editor, and its Color page can track a circular window onto a moving face: drop a Circle matte on the clip, blur what’s inside it, run the tracker forward, then step through and nudge any frames where the track slips. Render on the Deliver page. (Blackmagic’s page lists what the paid Studio edition adds on top — including Magic Mask and optical blur, its smarter assisted tools — so the free route is the manual one.)

Good: editors who already live in Resolve, one-off projects, zero budget.

Trade-off: manual and slow — expect to check and fix frames one by one, and nothing stops you from exporting before every frame is right.

Method 3: ffmpeg for static shots (the free one-liner)

If the camera never moves — tripod interview, fixed dashcam, doorbell cam aimed at a porch — and the face stays in one region, you don’t need tracking at all. A single ffmpeg command blurs a fixed rectangle for a time range:

ffmpeg -i input.mp4 -filter_complex \
"[0:v]crop=200:150:420:90,boxblur=10[b]; \
 [0:v][b]overlay=420:90:enable='between(t,12,38)'" \
-c:a copy output.mp4

That crops a 200×150 region at position (420, 90), blurs it, overlays it back on the original between seconds 12 and 38, and copies the audio untouched. Change boxblur=10 to a stronger radius for heavier blurring, or adjust the geometry for your frame. The filters are documented in the official ffmpeg filter reference.

Good: locked-off shots, scripted batch jobs, servers.

Trade-off: the box is dumb — the moment the face moves out of the rectangle, your redaction fails silently, which is the most dangerous failure mode there is.

Method 4: Mobile apps for quick social clips

For a 15-second vertical clip, a mobile blur app gets you done in minutes: point at the face, let the app’s tracker follow it, export.

Good: speed, zero learning curve.

Trade-off: many upload your video to their cloud for processing — fine for a meme, bad for anything sensitive. Watermarks are common, and tracking quality varies wildly between apps. The privacy risk is inverted: the clips where you most need a blur are exactly the ones you least want to upload.

When you need actual video redaction software

“Blurring a face for a vlog” and “redacting footage” get called the same thing, but they’re different bars. Redaction — the standard for legal holds, FOIA releases, HR investigations, research consent, insurance claims — means the redaction is complete, consistent across every frame, and reviewed by a human before it ships. That’s why the video redaction software category exists as its own tooling class, separate from effects apps: automatic detection so frames don’t get missed, a review queue so a human signs off, and on-device processing so the raw footage never leaves the machine that holds the responsibility. Why “offline” belongs on your checklist

A blurred face is still sensitive context: the frames around it show where you were, what was on the screens, who else was there. Uploading raw footage to a random web tool to blur one face adds a second copy of the most sensitive version of that video to a server you don’t control. FAQ

Does blurring a face in a video require motion tracking?

Only if the face moves. A locked-off camera with a subject in one spot can use a fixed blurred region (the ffmpeg method). The moment the person or the camera moves, you need tracking — manual in an editor, automatic in redaction software.

Can I blur license plates the same way?

Yes — plates are the same class of identifying information, and every method above works on them. Purpose-built tools detect plates automatically; in an editor you track a plate exactly like a face.

Is a blurred face recoverable?

A properly strong blur or pixelation applied to full frames is not practically reversible. A light blur can sometimes be partially attacked with AI enhancement tools, so for sensitive footage use a heavy blur or a solid black box — and check the output frame by frame, which is what a review queue is for.

What’s the difference between blurring and redaction?

Blurring is an effect; redaction is a process with a review step and a documented result. If the video could end up in a legal, HR, or compliance context, you want redaction — detection, human review, consistent application — not just a blur brush.
