Reverse SSH Tunnel on Mac: How It Works and How to Set One Up (2026)

Short answer: a reverse SSH tunnel lets a remote server reach a service that is running on your Mac. You open one with a command like ssh -R 8080:127.0.0.1:3000 user@your-server, after which requests to port 8080 on the server are carried through the SSH connection to port 3000 on your Mac. Starting one is a one-liner. The hard part is keeping it alive — macOS sleep, Wi-Fi changes, and dropped connections all kill it silently — and that is where most of this guide lives.

What a reverse SSH tunnel is

An SSH tunnel carries traffic through an encrypted SSH connection instead of exposing a port directly to the network. Direction is the only thing that separates the two kinds:

  • A local tunnel (ssh -L) — you connect from your Mac out to a service on a server. Classic case: reaching an Ollama instance or a database that only listens on the server itself.
  • A reverse tunnel (ssh -R) — the direction flips: the server reaches a service on your Mac. Your Mac keeps the service private; only the server’s forwarded port points at it.

That flipped direction is why it is also called remote port forwarding, and it solves a specific set of problems:

  • You are building on your Mac and need to show a running dev server to a teammate — through your own VPS, not a third-party tunnel service.
  • An external service needs to call a webhook (Stripe, GitHub, n8n) that has to reach your machine, and your Mac has no public address.
  • You want to reach a Mac at home from a server you control, without opening a single inbound port on the Mac’s router.

In all three cases the Mac initiates the outbound SSH connection — nothing needs to accept inbound connections on your side. The server, which does have a public address, donates one of its ports.

Set up a reverse SSH tunnel manually on macOS

The built-in ssh client handles everything. The basic form:

ssh -R 8080:127.0.0.1:3000 user@your-server

Read it right to left: connections to port 8080 on your-server are forwarded, through the tunnel, to port 3000 on your Mac (the target address is resolved from the Mac’s side). Log in, leave the window open, and the forward exists for as long as that session does.

Two practical additions turn that into the version worth using:

ssh -N -R 8080:127.0.0.1:3000 \
    -o ServerAliveInterval=30 \
    -o ServerAliveCountMax=3 \
    -o ExitOnForwardFailure=yes \
    user@your-server
  • -N — no remote shell; the session only carries the tunnel.
  • ServerAliveInterval / ServerAliveCountMax — the Mac pings the server every 30 seconds and gives up after three misses, so a dead tunnel is detected instead of pretending to live.
  • ExitOnForwardFailure=yes — if the server cannot create the forward (port already taken, for example), ssh exits with an error instead of staying connected without it.

Two limits worth knowing before you rely on this:

  1. The forwarded port binds on the server’s loopback by default. your-server:8080 works from the server itself; making it reachable from the outside world takes a server-side GatewayPorts setting in sshd_config — a deliberate security decision, not a client flag.
  2. The tunnel lives as long as the process. Close the laptop lid, switch from Wi-Fi to a hotspot, or lose the connection, and the forward is gone. -f backgrounds the session and autossh can restart it, and a launchd job can hold it open at login — it works, but now you are maintaining a small distributed system to share one port.

Why manual reverse tunnels break

Every failure above is survivable once. In practice they arrive together, on a schedule you do not control:

  • Sleep/wake. macOS suspends the ssh process; the server notices the dead connection and drops the forward. After wake, the forward does not come back on its own.
  • Network changes. Wi-Fi to Ethernet, Wi-Fi to hotspot — the TCP session dies and takes the forward with it.
  • Port conflicts on the server. Something else grabbed 8080 while you were gone. Without ExitOnForwardFailure, ssh stays connected and you get the worst outcome: a session that looks fine and forwards nothing.
  • No verification. The command returning success means the session started — not that your service actually answers through the tunnel. The failure you care about is silent.

That last one is the real cost. A reverse tunnel is infrastructure, and infrastructure you have to babysit is infrastructure you will find broken in front of an audience.

Frequently asked questions

What is a reverse SSH tunnel used for?

Exposing a service that lives on a machine without a public address: a dev server on your Mac shown to teammates through your VPS, a webhook target that external services can call, or a home machine you reach from a server. The public server donates its address; the private machine initiates the connection outward.

Is a reverse SSH tunnel safe?

The traffic is encrypted inside SSH, and by default the forwarded port binds only to the server’s loopback — reachable from the server, not the internet. It stays that way unless you deliberately change it: exposing the port publicly takes a server-side GatewayPorts change, and you should verify host keys so you are tunneling to your server and no other.

Reverse tunnel vs. local tunnel — what is the difference?

Direction. A local tunnel (-L) brings a remote service to your machine; a reverse tunnel (-R) makes a local service reachable from the remote side. Same encryption, opposite pointing.

Why does my reverse tunnel die when my Mac sleeps?

macOS suspends the ssh process and the connection underneath it times out; the server drops the forward, and nothing restarts it after wake. Keepalives make the death detectable, autossh or a launchd job can restart it, and a tunnel manager handles the reconnect as part of its job.
